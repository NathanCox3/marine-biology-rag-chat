Chapter 10 Deep Sea and Chemosynthesis

The deep sea is the largest habitat on Earth by volume, yet it receives little or no sunlight. Most deep-sea food webs depend on organic matter sinking from surface waters. This marine snow includes dead plankton, fecal pellets, molts, and other particles.

Hydrothermal vents and cold seeps support chemosynthetic communities. Instead of sunlight, microbes use chemical energy from compounds such as hydrogen sulfide or methane to produce organic matter. These microbes can live freely, form mats, or exist as symbionts inside animals.

Vent communities include tubeworms, mussels, clams, shrimp, crabs, and specialized fishes. Many vent animals depend on microbial symbionts for nutrition. Because vent habitats can be isolated and short-lived, dispersal and colonization are important ecological processes.

Deep-sea ecosystems are vulnerable to disturbance because many species grow slowly, reproduce late, and recover over long time scales. Mining, trawling, pollution, and climate-driven changes in food supply can affect deep-sea communities.
