Chapter 3 Mangroves and Seagrass Meadows

Mangroves are salt-tolerant trees and shrubs that grow along tropical and subtropical shorelines. Their roots stabilize sediment, reduce shoreline erosion, and trap organic matter. Mangrove roots also provide nursery habitat for juvenile fishes and invertebrates by creating shelter in shallow water.

Seagrasses are flowering plants that live in marine environments. They form underwater meadows in shallow coastal zones where sunlight reaches the bottom. Seagrass blades slow water flow, trap sediment, improve water clarity, and provide food and habitat for turtles, dugongs, fishes, mollusks, and crustaceans.

Mangroves and seagrass meadows are connected to coral reefs and other coastal ecosystems. Many fishes spend early life stages in mangroves or seagrass beds before moving to reefs as adults. This habitat connectivity increases survival and supports fisheries.

Both ecosystems store large amounts of carbon in plant biomass and sediments. This blue carbon storage makes them important in climate mitigation. However, coastal development, dredging, nutrient pollution, and physical disturbance can degrade or remove these habitats.
