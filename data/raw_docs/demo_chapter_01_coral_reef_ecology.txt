Chapter 1 Coral Reef Ecology

Coral reefs are biologically rich marine ecosystems built by reef-building corals and the calcium carbonate structures they produce. A reef creates many small habitats across its surface, including crevices, overhangs, lagoons, reef flats, and deeper slopes. These physical spaces shelter fishes, crustaceans, mollusks, worms, echinoderms, and many other organisms.

Reefs support biodiversity because they combine shelter, feeding opportunities, nursery habitat, and breeding sites in a compact area. Small fishes often use branching corals and rubble as refuge from predators. Larger predators patrol reef edges where currents deliver plankton and smaller prey. Many reef organisms also depend on tight ecological relationships, including grazing by herbivorous fishes that prevents seaweeds from overgrowing corals.

Coral polyps live in symbiosis with photosynthetic algae called zooxanthellae. The algae provide energy-rich compounds to the coral, while the coral offers protection and access to sunlight in shallow water. This relationship helps explain why many reefs form in clear, warm, shallow seas with enough light for photosynthesis.

Bleaching occurs when corals lose their symbiotic algae or when algal pigments decline under stress. Heat stress is a common cause, but pollution, sedimentation, disease, and changes in salinity can also contribute. If stressful conditions persist, bleached corals may die, reducing habitat complexity and affecting the wider reef community.
